SOFR, TRI-PARTY GC & THE SHORT END

Overnight secured funding rates and the front of the US Treasury curve. SOFR is the volume-weighted median of overnight Treasury-collateralized repo. The short-term bill and constant-maturity yields sit on top of that funding complex.

What is being plotted

SOFR (Secured Overnight Financing Rate) is the volume-weighted median rate on overnight Treasury-collateralized repurchase agreements across the tri-party, GCF, and FICC DVP segments. It is administered by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York and has been the primary USD overnight benchmark since the LIBOR transition.

TGCR (Tri-Party General Collateral Rate) is the volume-weighted median of overnight specific-counterparty tri-party GC repo secured by Treasuries (BNY Mellon data only; excludes GCF and Fed counterparties). It is a close cousin of SOFR and usually trades within a few basis points of it.

Short-term Treasury yields are the constant-maturity yields on 1-month, 3-month, 6-month, and 1-year notes/bills (FRED DGS1MO, DGS3MO, DGS6MO, DGS1). These are the cash market prices of short-duration US government debt.

All series are daily, percent. SOFR and TGCR begin in April 2018. Treasury CMT series have longer history; the charts focus on the overlapping period.

Levels — funding rates and the bill curve

The overnight secured rates and the short end of the Treasury curve move together because the repo market is the financing market for those securities. A dealer or money-market fund that buys a T-bill can finance it overnight in GC repo. Arbitrage keeps the bill yield close to the expected path of the overnight rate (adjusted for the small term premium and any specialness). When funding is scarce, both SOFR and bill yields rise; when reserves are abundant or the ON RRP is absorbing cash, the rates can compress toward the floor.

Spreads — how tightly they are bound

SOFR minus the 1-month Treasury yield is usually small and mean-reverting. Large positive spreads have appeared in funding stress (September 2019, March 2020, and episodes of QT-driven reserve scarcity). TGCR minus SOFR is typically a few basis points and reflects the composition difference (tri-party only vs. the broader SOFR universe). The 3-month and 6-month yields sit above the overnight rate by the amount of expected rate changes over that horizon plus a modest term premium.

Does SOFR “drive” the bond market?

Yes, at the front end, in a precise sense. The overnight secured rate is the foundation of the short-rate complex. SOFR futures and the SOFR swap curve price the market’s expected path of that overnight rate. That expected path is the dominant driver of the pricing of short-term fixed-rate instruments — T-bills, short coupons, and the short end of the swap curve. Longer-term yields add term premium, inflation compensation, and supply/demand for duration; they are not a mechanical function of today’s SOFR. But the entire curve is anchored by the expected path of the overnight funding rate that SOFR measures.

Policy works through this channel. The FOMC sets the target range for the federal funds rate and administers the interest on reserve balances (IORB) and the overnight reverse repo facility (ON RRP). Those administered rates bound the unsecured and secured overnight markets. SOFR, TGCR, and the bill curve then transmit the policy stance into the pricing of government debt and into the rest of the fixed-income market via the futures and swap curves.

Stress episodes make the transmission visible. In September 2019, SOFR spiked above 5% while the funds rate was still inside its target range; bill yields followed. In March 2020, the opposite occurred as the Fed flooded the system with reserves. In the 2022–2024 hiking cycle and subsequent QT, the bill curve tracked SOFR tightly while the longer end was more about the terminal rate and term premium.

01 // Description

This course builds on the models developed in the core FINTECH sequence and applies them to live fixed-income and commodity markets. The aim is threefold: to use those models as a portfolio manager would, to translate differences between model-implied value and observed market prices into investable positions, and to treat observed prices as information about the future path of policy, growth, inflation, and physical scarcity.

The fixed-income block covers the principal instruments (Treasuries, swaps, corporates, and related securitized products) and the quantities that define a price: yield, duration, convexity, and spread. Attention is given to the macroeconomic drivers of the curve and to market structure — cash versus futures versus swaps, dealer and non-dealer liquidity, RFQ and portfolio trading, TRACE, and the role of bond ETFs in price discovery. Students examine three families of practice that dominate electronic and levered rates and credit: discretionary relative-value, systematic factor and statistical-arbitrage approaches, and market-making / inventory.

The commodities and energy block treats listed futures and related paper as claims on delivery. Oil, gas, power, metals, and freight are studied through term structure as a storage-and-carry problem, through transformation spreads (crack, spark) that inherit industrial constraints, and through the basis between the contract and the physical good — grade, location, and timing. Students model a curve, follow a contract toward delivery, and analyze the effect on paper positions when inventory, logistics, or grid conditions fail to match the screen.

Throughout, work is quantitative and uses current market data. Students fetch and wrangle feeds from professional market systems with AI used freely for code, parsing, and first-pass analysis. Students are expected to move repeatedly from data and models to a market view, from that view to a position, and from the position to an explicit assessment of expected return and risk. By the end of the term students will be able to price rates and commodity instruments from live data and state, in numbers, what those prices imply for policy, growth, inflation, and scarcity from here into the future.

04 // Learning Objectives
01 Price a fixed-income instrument from live data: extract a yield, compute duration and convexity, and state what a change in the curve does to the price.
02 Treat SOFR futures and the swap curve as the living bond market. Separate the expected path of policy from the term premium, and say what the curve is claiming about the Fed from here.
03 Read a futures curve as a claim on delivery. Explain contango, backwardation, storage, carry, and convenience yield in numbers, and follow a contract as it approaches physical settlement.
04 Build and interpret transformation spreads (crack, spark) and the basis (grade, location, timing). Use netbacks and ratios to turn physical constraints into a paper position.
05 Move from a macro view (policy, growth, inflation, scarcity) to a position in rates or commodities, and report expected return and risk in numbers. Distinguish relative-value, systematic, and inventory/market-making approaches.
06 Use professional data systems and AI as tools. Remain responsible for the integrity of the pull, the model, and the position.
05 // Tentative Course Schedule
Class 01 — Curves, Term Premium, and Why These Markets Price the Same Way
  • Course setup and the three aims
  • A curve is a set of claims on the future; fixed income and commodities both live on one
  • Term premium in rates vs storage/carry in commodities — the shared structure
Class 02 — SOFR as the Living Bond Market and the Fed System
  • SOFR, the funds rate, and what the Fed actually sets
  • SOFR futures as the market’s path of policy
  • Reading a hike, a cut, and a pause from the strip
Class 03 — Duration, Convexity, and the Price of a Bond
  • Yield to price; modified and effective duration
  • Why long Treasuries can lose money in a hiking cycle
  • Convexity as the second-order correction
Class 04 — Cash, Futures, Swaps, and the Term Premium
  • Three ways to take the same view on the curve
  • Separating the expected path of short rates from the term premium
  • Swap spreads and when cash and paper diverge
Class 05 — Credit, Spreads, and Securitized Products
  • Corporate spreads and what they are pricing
  • Securitized products in brief (agency MBS, ABS) as curve + prepay / optionality
  • Relative-value in credit: on-the-run vs off, sector vs sector
Class 06 — Market Structure: TRACE, RFQ, Portfolio Trading, Bond ETFs
  • Dealer vs non-dealer liquidity
  • How prices are discovered in cash bonds
  • Three practices: discretionary RV, systematic factors, market-making / inventory
Class 07 — Midterm Review
Class 08 — Midterm Exam
Class 09 — Futures as Delivery Claims: Storage, Carry, and the Physical at Maturity
  • A futures contract is a bond that settles into something physical
  • Full carry, convenience yield, and the cost of holding the good
  • What happens to the paper when delivery is real
Class 10 — Term Structure as a Storage Problem
  • Contango, backwardation, and inventory
  • Roll yield and the cost of staying in the front
  • Modeling a curve and following a contract toward delivery
Class 11 — Transformation Spreads: Crack, Spark, and Chemical Constraints
  • Crack spreads and the refinery as a conversion plant
  • Spark spreads and the power plant
  • When industrial constraints (capacity, outages, weather) show up in the paper
Class 12 — Basis, Netbacking, and the Gap Between Screen and Physical
  • Grade, location, and timing basis
  • Netbacks: working backward from the product price to the raw material
  • What breaks when inventory, logistics, or the grid fail to match the screen
Class 13 — Ratios, Relative Value, and Macro-Driven Strategies
  • Ratios (gold/silver, crack, gas/power, etc.) as trades
  • Using macros (policy, growth, inflation, scarcity) to identify a view
  • From view to position to explicit expected return and risk
Class 14 — Putting It Together: A Position on the Curve
  • Design a rates or commodities position from the current tape
  • State what the prices imply for policy, growth, inflation, or scarcity
  • Risk the position (duration, curve, basis, inventory)
Optional class — Final review (after official classes end)
Final — date and time as posted in DukeHub

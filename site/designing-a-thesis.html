DESIGNING A THESIS

A thesis is a belief about the market that can be turned into a trading strategy. It must identify a specific risk that is over- or undervalued for a logical economic reason, estimate how long the mispricing will last, and supply a continuously updated tracker so you can see whether the idea still holds.

What makes a good thesis

A good thesis starts with a belief about the market or economy based on sound mercantile logic. It identifies a risk that is over- or undervalued because of a concrete economic reason — even if that risk is an abstract parameter such as vega or beta.

Examples:

  • If you believe a stock will rise, you are saying the market has overestimated the risk of the company declining in value. You accept that residual risk by buying the shares.
  • If you believe vega is overpriced, you might write options (accepting the risk that you are wrong). If you believe vega is underpriced, you might buy options.
  • If you believe a spread is under- or overpriced, you enter a pairs trade.

The thesis writer then asks a time-based question: how long until the market realizes its mistake and re-values the asset? You estimate this from logic and available data, and that estimate becomes the expected holding period.

Next, the mispricing must be quantified, plotted, and tracked. A live tracker (updated nightly or continuously) both validates that the insight is real and helps quantify how much value might be harvested. See the finished example from Nils at Built Beats Building.

Finally, the trader must consider the overall macro environment and how it will affect the trade.

Kill criteria

Write two or three observable conditions that would make you abandon the thesis. These are not vague feelings; they are numbers you can check.

Examples: the spread falls 15 % from its high since inception; beta to QQQ drifts above 0.3; the economic-surprise series no longer predicts the next month’s return; the rule’s equity curve underperforms cash for 20 consecutive trading days.

Estimating harvestable alpha

Look at the historical volatility or Sharpe of your tracker (even a rough one), rough turnover, and a simple cost assumption. The goal is not a precise number. It is to notice when an idea has a tiny edge after costs.

Different types of theses

Portfolio-based

Track risk contributions, correlations, or a simple multi-sleeve allocation versus a benchmark. You can also run different strategies (mean reversion, wheels) on multiple assets at the same time and watch the combined risk picture.

Starter tracker: a rolling correlation matrix or a risk-contribution pie versus a benchmark.

Technicals

Track a rule’s live or historical equity curve plus a regime filter. Be very careful to identify when and why the technical relationship can break.

Starter tracker: simple rule equity curve with an ATR-expansion filter that turns the rule off.

Macro / factor

Track a long/short factor or a simple economic-surprise versus price relationship.

Starter tracker: long/short quality-minus-growth or a basic economic-surprise versus subsequent return scatter.

Less motivated

Apply and extend one of the strategies we have already developed. Take the overnight or wheel code and add one observable condition that would turn it off.

Data and updates

Use what is already easy: course server or IBKR bars, LSEG, free FRED, Yahoo. The tracker should be able to update with a GitHub Action or a simple nightly script so it stays current while you are away.

Common failure modes

  • Vague theses that do not identify a specific mispriced risk.
  • Trackers that do not actually test the claimed belief.
  • No kill criteria.
  • Ignoring beta or costs.
  • Pure curve-fit with no regime awareness.

Homework checklist

  • One-sentence thesis
  • Time-horizon estimate
  • Tracker definition and chart
  • Two or three wrong-if conditions
  • Rough alpha / cost note
  • Macro-environment paragraph
  • Link to a live page (GitHub Pages is fine)

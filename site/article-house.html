I AM THE HOUSE NOW

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said it out loud. Then he doubled, and tripled, the government’s purchases of its own long-term bonds. This is what that trade is, who it is betting against, and why the cubes on this site say the risk he accepted is not small.

On September 8, 2026, at Southern Methodist University, Scott Bessent described his job in the language of the trading floor he came from:

“Whenever people say, Oh, well Treasury secretary is taking a risk, it’s my dream. I have asymmetric information. I am the house now. You can bet against me if you want.”

Quoted in the New York Times and the Wall Street Journal, September 9, 2026. He was talking about intervening in the yen with Japan, and about knowing what Japanese policymakers would do. The same week, the Treasury raised a long-bond buyback to as much as $6 billion — triple the usual size.

The hedge-fund trade, run from the Treasury

A hedge-fund manager who wants less exposure to rising long-term yields does a simple pair: own the long bond, fund it by selling (or issuing) the short end. If long rates go up, the long bond falls in price — unless you have already taken supply out of that market, or you are the bid. If short rates are something you can roll, you accept more frequent refinancing in exchange for less pain at the far end of the curve.

That is, in substance, what the Treasury has been doing. It is buying back older 10- to 30-year bonds — “liquidity support” in the official phrasing — and it still has to fund the government. The fluctuating cash need is typically met by Treasury bills, which mature in a year or less. Analysts immediately called it a Treasury version of Operation Twist: fewer long bonds in public hands, more short-term paper to roll. Bessent has said the buybacks are about making older bonds easier to trade, not about copying the Fed’s large-scale purchases. Both things can be in the press release. The arithmetic of the book is the same either way: duration risk is being pulled in; refinancing risk is being pushed out.

The scale is still small next to the stock. A $6 billion operation against tens of trillions of public debt will not, by itself, set the 10-year yield. The September 9 announcement did not even hold the market: long yields rose that day. The point of the article is not that $6 billion is a magic number. The point is the stance. Bessent has dropped the pretense that Treasury issuance is a neutral plumbing exercise. He is taking a view.

The view: Japan, and the long end

Japanese institutions have been, for decades, among the largest foreign holders of U.S. Treasuries. Their domestic alternative is Japanese government bonds. If the Bank of Japan raises its policy rate, Japanese yields rise. Money that had been earning more in the United States, after currency hedges, starts to look less attractive at home. A stronger yen also makes those hedges more expensive. The usual result, taught in every cross-border fixed-income desk, is less incremental demand for long-dated Treasuries and some upward pressure on U.S. long yields — not because Tokyo “dumps” the whole pile overnight, but because the next buyer is less eager.

Bessent said he has insight into what the Bank of Japan and Japanese policymakers will do. That is the “house” claim: he is not guessing the next Japanese meeting from a Bloomberg terminal. He is sitting in the room where the United States and Japan already intervened together in the yen, for the first time in years, in August 2026.

If you believe that Japanese demand for the long end is about to get worse, you do not wait to find out at auction. You try to absorb some long-end supply first — buybacks now — and you fund the government in bills, which you can roll, and which money-market funds will still bid for as long as they are paid. You are selling something you think you can live with (short-term debt) and buying something you think other people are about to want less of (long duration).

We do not split “Japan” out of foreign holdings on this site. The series we have is all foreign investors together. Japan is a large piece of that, not the whole. If Bessent’s Japan call is right, it is a headwind to the long end. If it is the whole story, it is probably too small a picture. Foreigners, in our data, have not been the main buyer of extra U.S. public debt lately. That is the next chart.

Who he is betting against

The Who is Financing the Treasury page splits every extra dollar of debt held by the public into the Fed’s own portfolio, foreign investors, and leftover private buyers in the United States (plus foreign buying that has not shown up yet). The leftover is a bucket. It is also, right now, most of the flow.

In 2020 the Fed was the buyer. In 2022–23 the Fed was a seller — shrinking its Treasury portfolio — and private buyers took the new debt and what the Fed was selling. In the latest year the leftover is still on the order of two-thirds of extra public debt. The Fed is a rounding error. Overnight cash that used to sit at the Fed has mostly gone; some of it bought bills, which still counts as leftover, not as the central bank.

So Bessent’s long-end bid is not crowding out the Fed. It is a small official bid sitting in front of a large private one. He is betting that private appetite for duration will get worse (Japan, term premium, whatever he sees) and that he can live with more bills. The people on the other side of that bet are whoever is in the magenta bars: money-market funds, banks, pensions, insurers, households — the same domestic bid that cleared quantitative tightening. If that bid holds, the House does not need to get bigger. If it doesn’t, buybacks of a few billion at a time will not be the whole story. They will be the start of a much larger official presence in the long end, run from the Treasury rather than the Fed.

The other side of the trade: fiscal risk

Duration risk is “what happens to the price of a long bond when long rates move.” Fiscal risk, here, is “what happens to the government’s own books when it leans on short-term debt and when interest already takes a large bite of incoming cash.” Bessent is trading the first for the second. The Sustainability Cube is the second.

Three things that cube tracks, in English:

  • How much of this quarter’s incoming cash is already spoken for by interest on existing debt.
  • How much more expensive new borrowing is than the rate already on the books — the refinancing gap.
  • How large the public debt is relative to the economy.

Those are not forecasts. They are the position. Shifting the book toward bills makes the refinancing gap matter more, not less: more of the debt comes due inside a year, so a rise in short rates hits the interest bill faster. Buying back longs can hold down the long yield that mortgages and corporate borrowers look at. It does not shrink the deficit. It does not retire the stock. It changes which rate and which holder the Treasury is married to.

The Fiscal Dominance Cube asks a related question: at what point does the Fed, looking at its own job, have to notice the Treasury’s financing problem. Bessent’s trade is a Treasury answer that tries not to wait for that moment — manage the long end yourself, keep the short end rolling.

He had other options

A Treasury secretary who worries about long yields is not required to become the house. The menu, honestly listed:

  1. Status quo. Keep issuing across the curve as the refunding announcements have done. Let the private leftover bid — the magenta bars — keep clearing. Do not pretend you can set the 10-year.
  2. Lock in duration the old-fashioned way. Issue more 10-year and 30-year bonds, not less. You pay today’s long rate for a long time. You accept that the long yield might be higher than you like, in exchange for not rolling a third of the book every year. That is the opposite of Bessent’s pair.
  3. Smaller deficits. Less new paper, period. Congress would have to do it. We will not pretend that is the live option this autumn.
  4. Become the house. Buy the long end, fund with bills, talk like a dealer who knows the other side’s cards. That is the choice.

The first two are boring. The third is politics. The fourth is a hedge-fund book run on a sovereign balance sheet. It can work if the leftover bid stays and if Japan is the main risk to the long end. It works less well if the leftover bid is the long end — if the people who have been taking extra public debt start to want a higher long yield as compensation, Japan or no Japan.

Is Japan the whole picture?

Probably not. Japan is a real channel and a real holder. It is also a country that already walked this path further than the United States has: a heavy government bond market, a central bank that became the bid, and a finance ministry that has spent decades managing duration for the state. When people say Bessent is borrowing Japan’s playbook, they mean official buying of the long end to keep yields from running. The uncomfortable comparison writes itself. The United States still has a large private leftover bid. Japan, for years, did not.

Our foreign-holdings series does not isolate Tokyo. If Japanese institutions step back, it will show up late, mixed with everyone else. What we can see without waiting on that revision is simpler: the Fed is not financing the extra debt. Private buyers are. Bessent is overlaying a small official long bid and a larger short-term issuance program on top of that. He is the house in the sense that he can see Japan and he can change the mix of the U.S. book. He is not the house in the sense that he can replace the magenta bars. Those bars are the people he is betting will still show up — or will show up less in longs and more in bills, which is the same bet written differently.

The cubes on this site exist so that bet has a scoreboard. The financing page exists so the counterparties have names, even if one of the names is “leftover.” Follow the links. The article is the story. The analyses are why it is allowed to be this sharp.

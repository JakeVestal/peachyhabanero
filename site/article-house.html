I AM THE HOUSE NOW

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said it out loud. Then he doubled, and tripled, the government’s purchases of its own long-term bonds. This is what that trade is, how much fiscal risk he is taking on for every unit of long-rate sensitivity he is taking out of the market, who he is betting against, and how we will know later if he won.

This piece is unfinished and is not linked from the rest of the site. If you have the URL, you are looking at a draft. Target: Monday.

On September 8, 2026, at Southern Methodist University, Scott Bessent described the job in the language of the trading floor he came from:

“Whenever people say, Oh, well Treasury secretary is taking a risk, it’s my dream. I have asymmetric information. I am the house now. You can bet against me if you want.”

Quoted in the New York Times and the Wall Street Journal, September 9, 2026. He was talking about intervening in the yen with Japan, and about knowing what Japanese policymakers would do. The next day, Treasury successfully purchased $5.2 billion of the announced $6 billion cap (triple the usual size) in off-the-run, less-liquid long bonds. Future operations for the remainder of the refunding quarter are set to maintain a minimum floor of $4 billion.

What he is actually doing (without the minus sign)

Bond desks talk about “duration” as if it were a thing you buy or sell. The word is doing too much work, and the formula everyone learns makes it worse:

ΔP ≈ −D × Δy

symbolmeaning
ΔPChange in the bond’s price
DDuration (a positive number: roughly, how many years of payments you are waiting on)
ΔyChange in yield, in decimal. Up means rates rose.
the minus signWhen yields go up, prices go down. That is the whole trick.

A 30-year bond has a large D. A three-month bill has a tiny D. If long-term yields rise one percentage point, the 30-year’s price falls a lot; the bill barely flinches. The minus sign is why “buying duration” sounds like you want rates to rise when you actually want the opposite if you own the bond.

Forget the slang for a minute. Bessent is doing two operational things:

  1. Buying back older 10- to 30-year Treasuries — taking long bonds out of public hands. Official phrasing: “liquidity support” for off-the-run longs. Arithmetic: fewer long bonds for the market to hold.
  2. Still funding the government, mostly in bills — paper that comes due in a year or less. Analysts called it a Treasury version of Operation Twist. Bessent has said the buybacks are not QE. Both can be in the press release. The book still shortens.

A hedge-fund trade, run by the Treasury

A hedge fund manager who wants less exposure to rising long-term yields does a simple pair: own the long bond, fund it by selling (or issuing) the short end. If long rates go up, the long bond falls in price — unless you have already taken supply out of that market, or you are the bidder. If short rates are something you can roll, you accept more frequent refinancing in exchange for less pain at the far end of the curve. In English, without “buying” or “selling” duration:

  • He is reducing how much the public book’s market value jumps around when long-term yields move. There is less 10- to 30-year paper sitting in private accounts to get hit when the 30-year yield ticks up. Long-rate price sensitivity of the outstanding stock goes down.
  • He is increasing how often the Treasury must refinance when short-term yields move. More bills means more of the debt is re-priced inside twelve months. Short-rate interest-bill sensitivity of the government goes up.

That is the pair. Hedge funds would say he is “long the long end, short the front.” The Treasury is not a hedge fund, but the secretary used to run one, and that is the book he just described. Long-rate pain for holders goes down. Refinancing pain for the government goes up. He is trading one kind of sensitivity for the other.

The scale of any single operation is still small next to the stock. $6 billion against tens of trillions will not, by itself, set the 10-year. On September 9 the announcement did not even hold the market: long yields rose that day. The point is the stance. Issuance is no longer being sold as a neutral plumbing exercise. He is taking a view.

The view: Japan, and the long end

Japanese institutions have been, for decades, among the largest foreign holders of U.S. Treasuries. Their domestic alternative is Japanese government bonds. If the Bank of Japan raises its policy rate, Japanese yields rise. Money that had been earning more in the United States, after currency hedges, starts to look less attractive at home. A stronger yen also makes those hedges more expensive. The usual result, taught on every cross-border desk, is less incremental demand for long-dated Treasuries and some upward pressure on U.S. long yields — not because Tokyo dumps the whole pile overnight, but because the next buyer is less eager.

Bessent said he has insight into what the Bank of Japan and Japanese policymakers will do. That is the “house” claim: he is not guessing the next Japanese meeting from a terminal. He is sitting in the room where the United States and Japan already intervened together in the yen, in August 2026, for the first time in years.

If you believe Japanese demand for the long end is about to get worse, you do not wait to find out at auction. You try to take some long bonds out of the float first, and you fund the government in bills that money-market funds will still bid for as long as they are paid.

We do not split “Japan” out of foreign holdings on this site. The series we have is all foreign investors together. Japan is a large piece of that, not the whole. If Bessent’s Japan call is right, it is a headwind to the long end. If it is the whole story, it is probably too small a picture. Foreigners, in our data, have not been the main buyer of extra U.S. public debt lately. That is the next chart.

Who he is betting against

The Who is Financing the Treasury page splits every extra dollar of debt held by the public into the Fed’s own portfolio, foreign investors, and leftover private buyers in the United States (plus foreign buying that has not shown up yet). Leftover is a bucket. It is also, right now, most of the flow.

Full method and the quarterly table: Who is Financing the Treasury. White = extra public debt. Cyan = Fed. Amber = foreign. Magenta = leftover.

In 2020 the Fed was the buyer. In 2022–23 the Fed was a seller — shrinking its Treasury portfolio — and private buyers took the new debt and what the Fed was selling. In the latest year leftover is still on the order of two-thirds of extra public debt. The Fed is a rounding error. Overnight cash that used to sit at the Fed has mostly gone; some of it bought bills, which still counts as leftover, not as the central bank.

So the long-end buyback is not crowding out the Fed. It is a small official bid sitting in front of a large private one. He is betting that private appetite for long bonds will get worse (Japan, term premium, whatever he sees) and that he can live with more bills. The people on the other side of that bet are whoever is in the magenta bars: money-market funds, banks, pensions, insurers, households — the same domestic bid that cleared the Fed’s runoff. If that bid holds, the House does not need to get bigger. If it doesn’t, a few billion at a time will not be the whole story. They will be the start of a much larger official presence in the long end, run from the Treasury rather than the Fed.

The price of the pair: fiscal risk per unit of sensitivity taken out

“He is taking duration out of the market” is a slogan until it has a denominator. “The cubes look worse” is a slogan until it has a numerator that moves because of this trade, not because the deficit exists.

The object that moves because he shortens the book is the extra interest the Treasury pays over the next twelve months on the larger pile of debt that comes due inside a year.

ΔI1y ≈ g × A0–1y

symbolmeaning
g Refi gap: the rate on new borrowing minus the rate already on the books, as a decimal. Same object as the Sustainability Cube’s refinancing axis.
A0–1y Principal that comes due inside a year. We have this as RESID_AMT_0_1Y from Treasury Fiscal Data, Table 3.
ΔI1y Extra interest, dollars, over the next twelve months if the gap stays where it is. This is the fiscal cost of a shorter book.

The other side is how much long-rate price sensitivity he took out. A buyback of B dollars of 10- to 30-year bonds, funded with bills, removes about

B × (Dlong − Dbill)

dollar-duration from the public float. D_long is large; D_bill is near zero. He is not “buying a negative number.” He is shrinking D of the paper the public still holds.

The bet ratio is

ρ = ΔI1y / (duration taken out of the public float)

In English: dollars of next-year interest per unit of long-rate price sensitivity removed from the market. If ρ is large, he paid a lot of incoming cash for a little relief at the long end. If ρ is small, the House is cheap. You can also quote ρ as percentage points of interest-versus-incoming-cash per year of average remaining maturity shortened — same story, in the units of the Sustainability Cube.

A $6 billion 10–20 year operation against a short wall measured in trillions is a tiny duration number and a real refinancing number. That is why the first operations looked small in yields. Size is part of the score, not a footnote.

Did he win, later

You cannot prove a counterfactual. You can pre-commit to what you will look at so “winning” is not a vibe. Two horizons: four quarters (was the twist even in the book?) and three years (did the Japan / long-end call pay?).

Bid Leftover still takes most extra public debt; long auctions do not gap Leftover fades or long tails blow; bills still bid. He moved the problem.
Size Operations scale with the miss (he said they could) without becoming the whole bid Ops stall at $6 billion and longs ignore them. September 9 already hinted.

Win, in one sentence: the long end is better than the Japan / term-premium tape said it should be, and the extra first-year coupon is smaller than that relief was worth.

Loss: longs did not care, and the short wall is bigger.

Japan is a channel, not the whole scoreboard. If leftover is ~70% of extra debt, the people who decide whether he won are in the magenta bars, not Tokyo.

He had other options

A Treasury secretary who worries about long yields is not required to become the house.

  1. Status quo. Keep issuing across the curve. Let leftover keep clearing. Do not pretend you can set the 10-year.
  2. Lock in the long rate the old-fashioned way. Issue more 10-year and 30-year bonds, not less. You pay today’s long rate for a long time. You accept a higher long yield than you like in exchange for not rolling a third of the book every year. That is the opposite pair: long-rate price sensitivity of the public book goes up; refinancing frequency goes down.
  3. Smaller deficits. Less new paper, period. Congress would have to do it. We will not pretend that is the live option this autumn.
  4. Become the house. Take longs out of the float, fund with bills, talk like a dealer who knows the other side’s cards. That is the choice.

The first two are boring. The third is politics. The fourth is a hedge-fund book run on a sovereign balance sheet. It can work if leftover stays and if Japan is the main risk to the long end. It works less well if leftover is the long end — if the people who have been taking extra public debt start to want a higher long yield as compensation, Japan or no Japan.

Is Japan the whole picture?

Probably not. Japan is a real channel and a real holder. It is also a country that already walked this path further than the United States has: a heavy government bond market, a central bank that became the bid, and a finance ministry that has spent decades managing the state’s remaining maturity. When people say Bessent is borrowing Japan’s playbook, they mean official buying of the long end to keep yields from running. The uncomfortable comparison writes itself. The United States still has a large private leftover bid. Japan, for years, did not.

Our foreign-holdings series does not isolate Tokyo. If Japanese institutions step back, it will show up late, mixed with everyone else. What we can see without waiting on that revision is simpler: the Fed is not financing the extra debt. Private buyers are. Bessent is overlaying a small official long bid and a larger short-term issuance program on top of that. He is the house in the sense that he can see Japan and he can change the mix of the U.S. book. He is not the house in the sense that he can replace the magenta bars. Those bars are the people he is betting will still show up — or will show up less in longs and more in bills, which is the same bet written differently.

The cubes exist so that bet has a scoreboard for the fiscal side. The financing page exists so the counterparties have names, even if one of the names is “leftover.” ρ is how expensive the pair is. The table above is how we will keep score. Follow the links. The article is the story. The analyses are why it is allowed to be this sharp.
